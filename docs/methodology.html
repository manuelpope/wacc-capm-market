Methodology

Every number the tool shows comes from one of four places: Yahoo Finance, a documented default, a cap that is applied on purpose, or the analyst model. This page says which is which, and why.

1. Where the inputs come from

Everything is pulled from Yahoo Finance through yfinance: the quote profile plus the quarterly income, balance and cash-flow statements, with the annual statements as a fallback.

InputHow it is computedFallback

Retention ratio 1 − (dividends ÷ net income) The last fiscal year, then 1.0 with a warning
Free cash flow The annual Free Cash Flow series, and the trailing twelve months Operating cash flow + capex
Why ROE is recomputed

Yahoo's returnOnEquity is inflated for companies that buy back stock — Apple reports around 142%. The tool therefore recomputes ROE from trailing net income over average book equity, which is closer to the textbook definition. It is still inflated for a company that is shrinking its equity, and the growth cap exists partly because of that.

Two data traps that are handled explicitly

^TNX is quoted in percent, but legacy Yahoo quoting multiplied it by ten (51.0 means 5.10%), so the value is normalized defensively. And a company that pays no dividend — Berkshire Hathaway — has no dividend row at all, which is not the same as a zero: the retention ratio falls back to 1.0 with an explicit warning.

2. The growth rate

By default g = ROE × retention ratio: the growth the current returns can fund from the profit it keeps. The raw figure is almost always unusable on its own, so it is capped twice, and every cap reports why it fired.

CapRuleWhy
The ceiling g ≤ 5% A perpetual rate above long-run nominal GDP is not sustainable.
The minimum spread WACC − g ≥ 3% A narrow gap makes the Gordon denominator explode and puts almost the whole value in the terminal value.

A manual growth rate is not subject to the ceiling — you are allowed to believe something else — but it still obeys the minimum spread, and the interface says that it was capped.

What the caps did in practice

The first run of this tool priced Apple at $2,190 a share against a $351 quote: a 10% growth ceiling and a 10.47% WACC left a 0.47% denominator and a terminal value of 234 times the cash flow. Exxon made the same point differently — a genuinely low beta of 0.14 gave a 5.62% WACC, and a 5% growth left a 0.62% spread, which put 97% of the enterprise value in the terminal value. After the fixes, Exxon's growth is 2.62% and its terminal value share is 87%, and both facts are reported.

3. The normalized free cash flow

The base cash flow is the average of the last three fiscal years, not the trailing twelve months. The trailing figure carries the noise of a single unusual quarter — a tax payment, a working capital squeeze, a restructuring — and the model then grows that number for five years.

Both figures stay visible in the interface. When they differ by more than 25% the tool says which way and by how much, and when the difference is extreme it says that the trailing figure looks like a cycle extreme and that the normalized base is the one to question.

The effect is not cosmetic: Apple's base drops from $136.06B to $114.54B and the value per share from $179.43 to $151.53, and Exxon's base drops from $42.96B to $29.26B.

Where the trailing figure comes from

The quarterly Free Cash Flow line, summed over the last four quarters. Some companies report fewer than four quarters, and a sum of fewer is not a year: the provider counts the quarters it used and says so in the warnings.

4. The valuation

A deliberately simple FCFF-style DCF:

FCF_t      = FCF_0 × (1 + g)^t
PV explicit = Σ FCF_t / (1 + WACC)^t          for t = 1..5
TV          = FCF_5 × (1 + g) / (WACC − g)
EV          = PV explicit + TV / (1 + WACC)^5
Equity      = EV − net debt
Value/share = Equity / shares outstanding

And the things the tool refuses to hide:

  • The terminal value share of the enterprise value is displayed. Above 85% the result lives almost entirely on the perpetuity assumption, and the tool says so.
  • A negative base cash flow reports no value per share at all. A growing perpetuity on a negative base is always negative: the number would be arithmetic, not valuation.
  • An extreme implied upside (more than 500%) is reported, because at that point the model is more sensitive to g and the WACC than to the price.
  • A negative equity value is reported: net debt exceeds the enterprise value.
