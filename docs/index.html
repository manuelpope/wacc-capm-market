WACC Lab

A FastAPI tool that computes the weighted average cost of capital, the sustainable growth rate and a deliberately simple DCF for any Yahoo Finance ticker, keeps the answers in SQLite, and shows you how much of the result depends on the two assumptions that carry the most weight.

Pick a ticker

A curated catalog of 69 well-known tickers, or any symbol Yahoo knows, typed by hand.

Compute

WACC, g and a value per share, with every input visible and every one of them overridable.

Persist

SQLite. A second request for the same ticker is served from the cache; a stale one is recomputed.

Question it

A WACC x g grid that shows how much of the answer is arithmetic and how much is assumption.

The flow

The whole thing is five steps, and each one is a separate unit that can be read on its own:

  1. The provider pulls the quote profile and the quarterly and annual statements from Yahoo Finance through yfinance.
  2. The store decides whether the snapshot is fresh, stale or missing, and fetches it again when it has to.
  3. The engines turn the raw facts into a WACC, a growth rate and a valuation. They are pure functions, and they never touch the network.
  4. The analyst, when Yahoo could not supply a number, asks a local model for the gap — and only the gap.
  5. The interface shows the result, the warnings, the news and the grid, and lets you override anything you disagree with.

What comes out

For a live analysis of Apple, the tool reported a WACC of 10.27%, a growth rate of 5% (capped, because the raw figure was 106%) and a value per share of $155.34 against a market price of $351.47. The last two facts are the interesting ones: the growth rate is not a forecast, and the gap between the model and the market is not an error — it is the output.

Read this first

This is a teaching-grade model on purpose: one cash flow base, one growth rate, one discount rate, one horizon. It is not a full three-statement DCF, and it says so. The honest list of what it cannot do is the most useful page in this wiki.

The three ideas

1. The caps are policy, and they are visible

A perpetual growth rate above long-run nominal GDP is not sustainable, so g is capped at 5%. A narrow gap between the discount rate and the growth rate makes the Gordon denominator explode, so at least 3% is kept between them. Both caps report why they fired, and both appear in the sensitivity grid as the fragile corner of the table.

2. The base cash flow is normalized

The valuation grows from the average of the last three fiscal years, not from the trailing twelve months. The trailing figure carries the noise of one unusual quarter, and the model then grows that number for five years. Both are shown, and when they differ by more than 25% the tool says which way and by how much.

3. A single number hides the interesting part

The WACC x g grid answers the question a single value per share cannot: how much of this number depends on the two assumptions that carry the most weight? For Apple the grid runs from $100.65 to $698.88 around a base case, and the most optimistic corners are exactly the fragile ones.
